## Executive Summary

Dark patches and uneven skin tone develop when the skin produces or deposits excess melanin in certain areas. The most common causes include sun exposure, inflammation from acne or injury (post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation), hormonal changes (melasma), genetics, and age. **The type of pigmentation determines which treatment is appropriate** — choosing the wrong approach can worsen discoloration rather than improve it.

## What Is Skin Pigmentation?

Skin pigmentation refers to the colour of your skin, which is determined by melanin — a natural pigment produced by specialised cells called melanocytes. When melanocytes become overactive or distribute pigment unevenly, areas of the skin can appear darker than surrounding tissue. [1,2]

**Pigmentation is not one condition.** The term covers several distinct disorders — including melasma, post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH), solar lentigines (sun spots), and freckles — each with different causes, depths, and treatment responses. [3,5,7,8]

---

## Why Does Skin Become Darker in Some Areas?

Skin becomes darker in specific areas when melanocytes — the pigment-producing cells in the epidermis — are stimulated to produce extra melanin. This can happen in response to:

- **Ultraviolet (UV) light** — the most consistent external trigger
- **Inflammation** — from acne, injury, eczema, or irritating skincare
- **Hormonal signals** — particularly oestrogen and progesterone
- **Genetic predisposition** — influencing baseline melanocyte activity

Instead of spreading evenly, the excess pigment can appear as defined patches, spots, or blotchy discoloration. [1,2,3]

**Pigment depth matters.** Some pigmentation sits mainly in the upper epidermis and may respond more predictably to treatment. Deeper dermal pigment can persist longer and is significantly more challenging to address. Post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation can involve melanin in the epidermis, dermis, or both — which is why two patients with similar-looking dark patches can respond very differently to the same treatment. [3,4]

## What Are the Most Common Causes of Pigmentation?

### 1. Sun Exposure → Solar Lentigines (Sun Spots / Age Spots)

UV radiation is one of the most consistent drivers of pigmentation. Chronic exposure stimulates excess melanin production and is strongly linked with solar lentigines — discrete, flat, brown spots that typically appear on sun-exposed areas such as the face, hands, and forearms. [1,7]

For patients in Malaysia, UV exposure is a year-round consideration. UV Index levels here regularly reach the "Very High" and "Extreme" categories, meaning daily photoprotection is not optional — it is clinically necessary. [9]

### 2. Inflammation → Post-Inflammatory Hyperpigmentation (PIH)

When skin experiences inflammation — from acne, eczema, insect bites, cosmetic procedures, or aggressive skincare — melanocytes can deposit extra pigment as a reactive response. The dark marks left behind are called post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation (PIH). [3,4]

PIH is particularly common and can be more persistent in darker skin types, including Malay, Chinese, and Indian skin. [3,4] This is clinically significant: treatments or procedures that cause heat, irritation, or trauma can *trigger new PIH* in susceptible patients if not carefully calibrated.

### 3. Hormonal Changes → Melasma

Melasma is a chronic acquired hyperpigmentation disorder characterised by symmetrical brown or grey-brown patches, most commonly appearing on the cheeks, forehead, nose bridge, and upper lip. It is strongly associated with:

- Oestrogen and progesterone (pregnancy, oral contraceptives, hormone therapy)
- UV and visible light exposure
- Genetic susceptibility [5,6]

Melasma is a **chronic and recurring condition**. It does not have a permanent cure, but can be effectively managed with the right combination of treatments and consistent sun protection. Patients who expect a one-time fix typically experience repeated recurrence and frustration. [5,11]

### 4. Genetics → Freckles and Some Lentigines

Freckles (ephelides) are small, flat, light brown marks that often appear in fair-skinned individuals with a genetic predisposition toward increased melanocyte activity. They typically darken with sun exposure and fade with less exposure. [8]

### 5. Age → Age Spots and Mixed Pigmentation

As skin ages, melanocyte distribution and activity become less regulated. This can result in irregular pigmentation, solar lentigines accumulating over decades, and the worsening of pre-existing concerns. [1]

## Why Does It Matter What Type of Pigmentation I Have?

**The type of pigmentation determines which treatment is appropriate.** Choosing the wrong approach can worsen discoloration instead of improving it.

Solar lentigines, PIH, freckles, and melasma can all appear as brown discoloration — but they behave differently. Melasma, for example, can be significantly worsened by heat, certain laser wavelengths, and aggressive procedures that would be appropriate for treating solar lentigines. [3,5,7,8]

This is especially important for Asian and darker skin types (Fitzpatrick III–V), where aggressive treatment or inflammation can trigger *new* PIH — a phenomenon called treatment-induced PIH. PIH affects darker-skinned patients with greater frequency and severity than lighter skin types. [3,4]

**Copying a treatment that worked for someone else is one of the most common reasons pigmentation treatment fails** — or produces a result that is worse than the starting point.

## What Should I Do If I Have Dark Patches or Uneven Skin Tone?

### Step 1: Observe before you treat

Note where the patches are, how long you have had them, and whether anything seems to trigger or worsen them — such as sun exposure, menstrual cycle timing, specific products, pregnancy, or hormonal medication changes.

### Step 2: Start sun protection now

UV and visible light exposure worsen almost every type of pigmentation and contribute to recurrence after treatment. Daily broad-spectrum SPF is the first line of management, regardless of what treatment follows. The American Academy of Dermatology states that treatment for dark spots and patches begins with sunscreen. [10]

### Step 3: Be cautious with untested products and procedures

Strong actives, harsh exfoliation, or salon procedures applied without knowing your pigmentation type can inflame the skin and worsen PIH-prone patients. [3,4]

### Step 4: Get a proper assessment before any treatment

If your pigmentation is spreading, changing, recurring after treatment, or simply not improving despite consistent skincare, a doctor's assessment is the most important next step.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Are dark patches and uneven skin tone the same thing?

Not always. Uneven skin tone is a general term for blotchiness or discoloration across a larger area, while dark patches usually refer to more defined areas of excess pigment. Both involve excess melanin, but the underlying type can differ significantly. [1]

### Can dark patches go away on their own?

Some can fade gradually over time, particularly epidermal PIH after mild inflammation. However, the timeline varies widely — epidermal PIH may take months to years to resolve without treatment, deeper pigment can be more persistent, and melasma commonly recurs, especially with ongoing sun exposure. [4,10,11]

### Will skincare alone fix my pigmentation?

Skincare with active ingredients (such as tranexamic acid, niacinamide, alpha-arbutin, or azelaic acid) combined with daily sun protection can help improve certain types of pigmentation over time. However, persistent, recurrent, or deeper pigmentation — particularly melasma or mixed-depth PIH — usually requires medical assessment and often a combination of in-clinic treatments alongside a home skincare regimen. [10]

### Why does my melasma keep coming back after treatment?

Melasma is a chronic hyperpigmentation disorder with a strong tendency to recur, particularly with UV and visible light exposure, hormonal fluctuations, and heat exposure. Successful melasma management is not a one-off course of treatment — it requires ongoing photoprotection, potentially maintenance therapy, and a long-term management strategy. [5,11]

### Is laser treatment suitable for all types of pigmentation?

No. Laser selection depends heavily on the type, depth, and colour of pigmentation, as well as the patient's skin type. Certain laser wavelengths can worsen melasma or trigger PIH in darker skin types. This is why pigmentation type and skin type assessment must precede any laser recommendation.

### Is pigmentation treatment safe for darker skin tones?

With appropriate assessment and calibration, many pigmentation treatments are safe for darker skin types. However, the risk of treatment-induced PIH is meaningfully higher in Fitzpatrick III–V skin types. This requires clinicians experienced with Asian and darker skin to select the right treatment, energy level, and sequencing. [3,4]

### Is pigmentation dangerous?

Most common pigmentation concerns are benign and cosmetic in nature. However, any pigmented lesion that changes in size, shape, or colour, has irregular borders, or looks different from surrounding spots should be assessed by a doctor to exclude more serious conditions. The American Academy of Dermatology recommends using the ABCDE criteria (Asymmetry, Border, Colour, Diameter, Evolving) to identify potentially concerning pigmented lesions. [13]

### How long does pigmentation treatment take?

This depends on the type and severity of pigmentation. Epidermal PIH may show meaningful improvement within 8–12 weeks of a consistent treatment plan. Melasma management is typically measured in months to years with maintenance. A realistic timeline is one of the first things the medical team at Klinik Dr Diana communicates during assessment.

### What is the difference between melasma and PIH?

Melasma is a chronic hormonal and UV-driven pigmentation disorder that typically appears in symmetrical patches on the face. PIH is reactive pigmentation that appears after inflammation or injury — commonly after acne, eczema, or procedures. They can coexist and can look similar, which is one reason visual assessment alone is insufficient for treatment planning. [3,5]

### What is tranexamic acid and why is it used for pigmentation?

Tranexamic acid is a compound that inhibits plasmin activity in the skin, which in turn reduces tyrosinase activation — a key enzyme in melanin production. It is used topically, orally, or via injection in pigmentation management, particularly for melasma, and has a comparatively favourable safety profile for darker skin types.
